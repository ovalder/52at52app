Season 1 taught me something that matters more than any finish time: I would not have stayed nearly as active without the challenge. Giving myself a commitment, and a reason to show up every week, made a real difference. When those 52 half marathons were done, I wanted to keep that going.

But continuing did not have to mean repeating. I wanted a new challenge, not a rerun of the same year.

## Bringing the bike back

I love riding my bike. During Season 1, running took priority so often that I really missed cycling. Season 2 makes room for both: runs, rides, or a mix of the two, spread over as many activities and days as the week allows.

The weekly target stays at **52 credited kilometers**, from Saturday to Friday. Because I generally find cycling easier per kilometer than running, I am giving bike distance a 2:1 conversion: two kilometers ridden count as one kilometer toward the challenge.

<div class="challenge-formula"><p>Run km + (ride km ÷ 2) ≥ 52</p></div>

| My week | Actual distance | Credited toward the goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Running only | 52 km running | 52 km |
| Cycling only | 104 km cycling | 52 km |
| A bit of both | 26 km running + 52 km cycling | 52 km |
| Another mix | 40 km running + 24 km cycling | 52 km |

The activity data will still show the distance I actually covered. The conversion only applies to the weekly challenge score, not to pace, speed, elevation or equipment mileage. A missed week stays missed; there is no carrying extra kilometers into another week.

## Why not 53 at 53?

A few friends asked why I am keeping the 52 when I am turning 53. My answer: I have created a brand now, and I want to stick to it! Besides, a year gives me 52 full weeks to work with. That is a convenient excuse, and I am taking it. LoL.

## The thinking behind 2:1

This is a practical rule for my challenge, not a claim that every 104 km ride is physiologically identical to a 52 km run. In my own training, long runs and harder rides have often averaged around 155 bpm. That is useful personal context, but I am not treating a matching heart rate as proof of identical energy expenditure.

The **2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities** assigns different MET values to activities and intensities; these are useful comparisons, not precise calorie measurements for an individual ([Herrmann et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)). Its cycling table classifies riding at 14–15.9 mph, approximately 23–26 km/h, as vigorous effort, which puts my roughly 25 km/h riding pace in a different category from a casual cruise ([2024 Compendium activity table](https://pacompendium.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2024-adult-compendium_1_2024.pdf)).

Real-world cycling power also depends on aerodynamic drag, air velocity, gradients and rolling resistance, not simply the number of kilometers covered ([Martin et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28121252/)). That is another reason I want a consistent challenge rule rather than pretending every route has the same energy cost.

Scott and colleagues also showed why comparing running and cycling involves more than a single oxygen-uptake measurement: in their study, combined estimates of aerobic and anaerobic expenditure were similar for work-matched, one-minute bouts of cycling and uphill running ([Scott et al., 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1334197/)). Those were brief laboratory efforts, not long outdoor sessions, and the study does not establish a 2:1 distance conversion ([Scott et al., 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1334197/)).

So I am using the research as context, not as a certificate of exact equivalence. Two-to-one is my simple, consistent way to make room for the bike while keeping the weekly commitment meaningful. The goal is the same as before: keep moving, keep showing up, and enjoy the journey.
